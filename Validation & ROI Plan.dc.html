1. The three-layer rule

Every card carries at most three proof objects, and never a bare adjective. If a layer has no source, the card says so out loud rather than implying one.

Metric
One number with a named origin — a customer baseline, a platform fact, or a benchmark range from the ROI tool. Ranges beat point estimates: 5–15% reads as honest, 12.4% reads as invented.
Testimony
A verbatim quote with speaker, title, company, and source. Anonymized to role + industry when the customer hasn't cleared their name.
ROI math
A formula taking one or two inputs from the prospect profile, producing a dollar line that drops into the running ledger. Shown as low / likely / high, never a single number.

3. Live ROI calculator — architecture

One discovery profile is captured once, at the start of the walkthrough. Every card then reads only the inputs it needs and contributes one line to a running ledger. Nothing is entered twice, and the total at the end is the sum of the lines the rep actually showed.

Profile — captured once
Annual freight spend by mode — parcel, LTL, FTL, ocean, air
Annual shipment count by the same five modes
Shipping/logistics headcount and loaded hourly rate
Minutes spent per shipment today, end to end
Carrier invoices processed per month
Dock appointments per week; number of docks
Locations, and owned trucks if any
Incumbent TMS annual cost, if replacing one
Three bands, always
Each line computes low / likely / high straight from the ROI tool's percentage ranges. The ledger totals all three. The pitch quotes the low number and lets the high number sit visible next to it.
Every line is defeasible
A prospect can zero out any lever on the spot — "we already audit invoices" — and the total re-sums. A model that survives being argued with is the one that gets forwarded to the CFO.
No double counting
Freight-cost levers apply to mode spend once. Consolidation, pooling and mode-shift all reduce the same LTL spend, so they share a single capped pool rather than stacking.
Two profiles, not one
Hero Bread taught us that a team of one has no labor to save. A "cost reduction" profile hides labor lines entirely and models freight, error and claim recovery only.
Ledger behavior during the pitch
A persistent strip shows Savings identified so far and grows as cards are visited, with the card name attached to each line. The closing screen lists every line with its source, the three-band total, subscription cost against it, payback in months, and a one-click export. Buffalo Seal's model ran 5.9× ARR — that ratio is the shape to aim for, stated as a ratio rather than a promise.

5. Gaps to close, in order

  1. Strip the two unsourced numbers — the 40% on-time claim and any use of "up to 30%" as a working assumption. These are the only things in the deck that can be falsified in the room.
  2. Fix the two structural defects first — swapped OMS 03/04 animations and the orphaned AutoDispatchDemo. Validation attached to the wrong animation is worse than none.
  3. Run three customer interviews. Alley-Cassetty (Bill Westman has already volunteered as a reference and spoken to a prospect), Callaway Blue at 90 days, Buffalo Seal at one year against the $140,294 model. Ask for four numbers each: minutes per shipment before and after, status calls per day before and after, invoice errors caught per month, and freight spend change per mode.
  4. Get one measured case study published. Every intake we have grades Quantified Results 1/5; that is the single biggest hole in the pitch.
  5. Add the cost-reduction profile so small-team prospects don't get a labor-savings model that reads as irrelevant — the documented Hero Bread failure.
  6. Wire the ledger. Once numbers are agreed, the calculator is mechanical: a profile object, one formula per card, one summing strip, one closing screen.
